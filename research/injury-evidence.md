# Evidence note: beginner running injury risk

Research backing for the explainer's core message: running is easy and cheap to
start, but beginners can underestimate the need to train gradually, manage
workload, recover, and adapt. Not part of the built site — a working note for
whoever writes the copy.

Scope: this is a small, focused set of sources chosen because they're
systematic reviews, peer-reviewed, or from a recognised public-health body —
not an exhaustive literature survey.

## 1. Novice runners have a higher running-related injury (RRI) rate than more experienced runners

**Claim we can safely make:** In the research literature, novice runners
consistently show a higher rate of running-related injury per hour of running
than recreational/experienced runners — roughly double, across the studies
pooled in this review.

**Finding:** Weighted injury incidence of **17.8 per 1000 h of running**
(95% CI 16.7–19.1) in novice runners vs. **7.7 per 1000 h** (95% CI 6.9–8.7) in
recreational runners.

**Source:** Videbæk S, Bueno AM, Nielsen RO, Rasmussen S. "Incidence of
Running-Related Injuries Per 1000 h of Running in Different Types of Runners:
A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *Sports Medicine* 2015;45(7):1017–1026.
<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4473093/>

**Caution:**
- This is a **rate per hour of running exposure**, not a per-person
  probability. Do not turn "17.8 per 1000 h" into "you have an X% chance of
  getting hurt." It describes study populations, not an individual.
- The included studies didn't share one strict definition of "novice" (mostly:
  no regular running in the prior ~12 months) or one definition of "injury"
  (some used 1-day time loss, others 1-week, others "sought medical
  attention"). The review itself flags this heterogeneity as a limitation —
  treat "novice runners get hurt roughly twice as often as recreational
  runners" as the robust takeaway, not the exact decimal figures.

## 2. Most commonly affected body areas: knee, lower leg, ankle/foot

**Claim we can safely make:** Running-related injuries are concentrated at or
below the knee — the knee, lower leg, and ankle/foot region account for the
largest share of reported injuries in the research literature.

**Finding:** Knee, ankle, and lower leg had the highest proportion of injury
**incidence**; knee, lower leg, and foot/toes had the highest proportion of
injury **prevalence**. Overall injury incidence across included studies was
40.2% (± 18.8), prevalence 44.6% (± 18.4) — population-level review estimates,
not an individual's odds. The most common specific conditions were things like
Achilles tendinopathy, medial tibial stress syndrome ("shin splints"),
patellofemoral pain, and plantar fasciitis — overuse conditions, not acute
trauma.

**Source:** Kakouris N, Yener N, Fong DT-P. "A systematic review of
running-related musculoskeletal injuries in runners." *Journal of Sport and
Health Science* 2021;10(5):513–522.
<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2095254621000454>

**Caution:**
- **This review is about running-related musculoskeletal injuries** — mostly
  overuse conditions (tendinopathy, stress syndromes, patellofemoral pain) —
  **not** long-term joint degeneration or osteoarthritis, which is a separate
  body of research this review doesn't speak to. "Knee is the most commonly
  injured site" must not be written or read as "running damages your knees" —
  those are different claims about different things (an overuse injury you can
  recover from, vs. degenerative joint disease).
- Incidence/prevalence percentages here summarise pooled study data with wide
  variation between studies (± is the between-study spread) — again, not an
  individual's risk.

## 3. Reputable guidance: increase training gradually, build in recovery

**Claim we can safely make:** Recognised public-health guidance for beginner
runners recommends increasing running time/distance gradually and treating
rest days and warm-up/cool-down as a required part of the plan, not optional
extras.

**Finding (NHS Couch to 5K):** A 9-week, 3-times-a-week plan that alternates
walking and running, gradually increasing continuous running time to 30
minutes by the end. Explicit guidance: "Rest days give your body time to
recover, help prevent injury, and are just as important as the runs
themselves" — with a rest day recommended between every run. Every session
includes a 5-minute warm-up and cool-down walk. Stretching after the cool-down
"helps your muscles recover" and "may help reduce your risk of injury." Users
are explicitly told it's fine to repeat a week if they're not ready to
progress.

**Source:** NHS. "Couch to 5K running plan." NHS Better Health.
<https://www.nhs.uk/better-health/get-active/get-running-with-couch-to-5k/couch-to-5k-running-plan/>

**Caution:** This is public-health behavioural guidance (a recommended
programme), not a clinical trial outcome — cite it as an example of what a
recognised public-health body recommends, not as injury-rate evidence.

### Supporting research angle: training load as a risk factor

**Claim we can safely make:** Research on risk factors for running injuries
points to training/running characteristics — including how quickly weekly
volume increases — as a contributing domain, which lines up with the
"progress gradually" guidance above, but the certainty of this specific
evidence is low.

**Finding:** An umbrella review (a review of reviews) found running/training
characteristics — weekly distance, training volume, starting a running
programme — showed "medium to large associations" with injury risk across the
13 systematic reviews it synthesised.

**Source:** Correia CK, Machado JM, Dominski FH, de Castro MP, Fontana HB,
Ruschel C. "Risk factors for running-related injuries: An umbrella systematic
review." *Journal of Sport and Health Science* 2024;13:793–804.
<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11336318/>

**Caution:** The authors rate **all 13** of the reviews they synthesised as
"critically low" or "low" quality (AMSTAR 2), state results "should be
approached cautiously in clinical practice," and note the reviews barely
overlap in which underlying studies they draw on. Use this only to say
"training-load progression is a plausible, actively-researched risk factor" —
not as strong proof of a specific rule (e.g. don't use it to justify a precise
figure like "the 10% rule"; a scan of practitioner sources found that specific
figure is a popular heuristic without solid evidencey backing).

## Wording checklist for the explainer (from the brief)

- [ ] Never say running generally damages knees — overuse injury ≠ joint
      degeneration.
- [ ] Keep running-related injuries (acute/overuse, often recoverable)
      separate from long-term joint degeneration (a different, unaddressed
      question here).
- [ ] State findings as population/study statistics, not as "you will" or "you
      have an X% chance."
- [ ] No sudden cardiac death, no comparison with other sports — out of scope
      for this explainer.
